# Prose Authoring

Build instructional prose collaboratively. Align on the teaching sequence before drafting, then work through the approved structure one section at a time.

## Workflow

1. Establish the intended reader, the problem they are trying to solve, and what they should understand or be able to do after reading. Infer these from context when they are already clear.
2. Propose an outline before writing article prose. For each section, state the question it answers or the takeaway it delivers. Include planned visuals, examples, or TODOs when they affect the teaching sequence.
3. Ask the user to approve or revise the outline. Do not draft the article until the outline is approved.
4. After approval, create the document skeleton with the agreed headings and any known asset TODOs. Preserve the approved order.
5. Draft one section at a time. Ask for feedback after each section and wait for sign-off before drafting the next one.
6. Within a section, revise only the paragraph or idea under discussion unless a related transition must change. Do not churn prose the user has already approved.
7. After all sections are approved, make a continuity pass for repeated ideas, missing transitions, inconsistent terminology, and conclusions that do not follow from the preceding explanation. Keep approved substance intact unless a correction is necessary.

If the user explicitly requests a complete draft in one pass, follow that instruction instead of imposing review gates.

## Teaching Sequence

Organize each article around the reader's goal, not around a catalog of facts or terminology. A useful default sequence is:

1. The outcome the reader is trying to achieve.
2. What happens during the process and why it matters.
3. The progression the reader can observe, feel, or measure.
4. The methods available for reaching the target and their practical tradeoffs.
5. Exceptions, adjustments, and more advanced terminology once the default case is clear.
6. What the reader should learn or try next.

Change this sequence when the subject demands it, but make every section earn its place in the reader's progression.

## Prose Principles

- Start sections with the reader's purpose or immediate question. Avoid generic openings such as "a recipe can give" or abstract claims that do not yet help the reader decide or act.
- Introduce a concept only when the reader needs it to understand the current step. Do not mention a misconception, alternative framing, or vocabulary concern that the reader had no reason to consider.
- Use technical terms contextually. Show the progression or behavior first, name the term where it becomes useful, and define it through its role in the process.
- Prefer observable evidence over detached definitions. Describe what changes, what the reader should look for, and what that change means for the goal.
- Explain the need before introducing a tactic. Present a method such as a rest, temperature adjustment, or test only after the reader understands the problem it solves.
- Distinguish the target from the method. Time ranges and equipment settings are checkpoints; the relevant behavior or result determines completion.
- Present the common target first, then explain meaningful exceptions and why they differ. Do not turn a useful test or technique into a universal rule.
- Remove redundant stages and claims that are accurate but poorly timed. Narrative flow matters more than including every related fact.
- Prefer connected paragraphs when the material describes a progression. Use lists or glossary-style definitions only when comparison or lookup is genuinely the reader's task.
- End with a clear next step or adjacent lesson so the reader knows how to continue building understanding.

## Feedback Discipline

Treat feedback as evidence about the article's teaching logic, not merely as a request to swap words. Before replacing weak prose, identify the job that sentence or paragraph should perform in the sequence. Apply the resulting principle to the section being revised, while preserving the user's approved voice, scope, and factual choices.
